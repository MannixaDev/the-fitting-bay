01 How to take the measurements

Wrist-to-floor

Stand upright on a hard, flat floor in the shoes you play in. Let your arms hang completely relaxed at your sides. Don’t stretch down, don’t shrug up. Measure from the crease where your palm meets your wrist straight down to the floor. Have someone else read the tape: bending to look at it yourself changes your posture and typically adds an inch.

Typical range is 27"–40" (69–102 cm). This measurement is the reason two golfers of the same height can need lie angles two degrees apart. It captures arm length, which height alone can’t.

Hand length (for grip size)

Palm up, measure from the crease of your wrist to the tip of your middle finger. Keep the tape flat on the palm. Don’t wrap it over the fingertip or around the heel of your hand.

Height

Back to a wall, heels down, looking straight ahead. Measure in golf shoes if you can, or add ½" if you can’t. The chart assumes you’re standing as you stand over the ball.

02 Lie angle — the Bay Scale, and what a degree is worth

Lie angle is the angle between the shaft and the ground when the sole sits flat. If it’s wrong, the sole isn’t flat at impact, the effective loft twists, and the ball starts offline before the face angle gets a say.

Rule of thumb: every 1° of lie error costs you about 3 yards on a mid-iron. Roughly a third of that’s the ball starting offline; the rest is the curve it puts on the shot, because the face is now closed or open to a path that hasn’t moved.

Why wedges punish it, and why the usual explanation is wrong

You’ll read everywhere that a lie error costs you more yards as loft goes up. It doesn’t. The yards are remarkably flat across the bag, because the bigger face error in a wedge is almost exactly cancelled by the shorter shot. What actually changes is the size of the face error itself:

face change = arctan( sin(lie error) × tan(loft) )

ClubLoftCarry Face turned by 1° of lie errorOffline As % of the shot

A degree of lie error turns a 60° wedge face four and a half times as far as a 4-iron face. The ball finishes a similar distance offline either way, but as a share of the shot it’s nearly three times worse, on the club you’re trying to hit closest. That’s the real reason wedge lie matters, and it’s why a fitter checks your wedges separately from your set.

The face-angle figures are geometry and are exact. The yardages are modelled: start direction is about 75% face for an iron, and the curve is anchored on published TrackMan face-to-path data. Treat the yards as indicative and the ratios as solid.

How the Bay Scale works

Every height has a reference wrist-to-floor: the arm length that plays a standard lie. The scale is simply how far you sit from it:

your wrist-to-floor − reference for your height = your code
1 inch of deviation = 1 degree of lie

Measure high for your height and your arms are short: your hands sit high at address and the club needs to be more upright (U). Measure low and your arms are long: hands low, club needs to be flatter (F). Land on the reference and you’re LEVEL.

The codes are the answer, not a lookup key. U2 is two degrees upright. We also report the unrounded figure, so you can see whether you sit in the middle of a band or on its edge, which is exactly the case where a fitter would test both.

CodeLie vs standardWrist-to-floorMiss it produces if this is wrong for you
Too upright → the toe sits up, the face points left at impact → high pulls and hooks. Too flat → the heel sits up, the face points right → low pushes and slices. That relationship is what a fitter is reading on a lie board.

Static charts get you a starting point. A dynamic fit — hitting off a lie board with impact tape on the sole — gets you the answer, because your posture and shaft droop at impact aren’t visible in a tape measurement. The two commonly differ by one step on the scale.

Length comes from height, in the half-inch steps club builders actually work in:

A second, independent opinion comes from wrist-to-floor alone: under 32" suggests −1", 32"–34" suggests −½", 34"–36" is standard, 36"–37.5" suggests +½", above 37.5" suggests +1". When the two disagree it means your arms are long or short for your height, and the tool says so rather than hiding it.

Changing length changes lie, and by more than most people expect. The clubmaking rule is 1° for every ½" of length, so an inch is worth two degrees. A longer club plays more upright, because soling it with your hands at the same height lifts the toe; a shorter club plays flatter. That’s why "just get them cut down" is rarely just that: take an inch off and you’ve also moved the lie two degrees flat, which is two full steps on the Bay Scale. A good builder bends the set back; a cheap job doesn’t.

Roughly: 130–145 yd → A; 145–160 yd → R; 160–175 yd → S; 175 yd+ → X. Less reliable than a speed reading, because carry distance mixes clubhead speed with how well you strike it.

Weight matters more than the letter

Flex isn’t standardised. One brand's S plays like another brand's R, and the same shaft in two weights behaves like two different shafts. Weight is what drives your tempo, your ability to find the centre of the face, and your consistency swing to swing.

ShaftTypical weight rangeSuits
Driver graphite40–80 gLighter for slower/smoother; heavier for fast, aggressive transitions
Iron graphite50–105 g7-iron speed under 70 mph, age 60+, joint issues, or deliberate weight reduction
Iron steel95–130 g7-iron speed 70 mph and up; more feedback and a tighter dispersion

Tempo overrides the chart at the edges. A fast, aggressive transition loads a shaft like extra speed does; a slow, smooth one unloads it. If you’re within a few mph of a flex boundary, tempo decides which side you land on.

It’s common and correct to play one flex softer in irons than in the driver. Iron shafts load differently and the shorter length makes the same flex play stiffer.

Angle of attack changes everything. Two golfers at the same speed can need three degrees of different loft. Hitting down on the driver costs launch and adds spin. You need more loft to compensate. Hitting up (+3° to +5° is the tour ideal) adds launch for free, so you can play less loft and get more carry from the same speed.

Length

Stock drivers ship at 45.5"–45.75". Tour average is closer to 44.5"–45". Shortening a driver costs roughly 2–3 yards of theoretical distance and usually returns more than that in centre-face contact, because the face is only about 4" wide and it’s much easier to find the middle of it with a shorter club. If you’re choosing between the two, take the shorter one.

Grip size is a ball-flight tool, not just a comfort one. Bigger grips quieten the hands and slow face rotation. They help a hook. Smaller grips let the hands release. They help a slice. If your measurement and your miss disagree, the miss usually wins.

Build-up tape adds diameter at the butt but not much in the lower-hand area, so two wraps isn’t the same as a true midsize grip. And grip weight counts: going from a 50 g grip to a 66 g midsize changes swing weight by about two points.

08 Wedges — loft gapping, bounce and grind

Gapping

Space your wedges 4–6° apart, which is roughly 10–15 yards. Start from your actual pitching wedge loft, not from a nominal "PW". If your set PW is 43°, a 56° sand wedge leaves a 13° hole. You need something around 48° and 52° in between.

Bounce

Low, 4–8°Shallow attack angle, firm turf, tight lies, links golfSoft turf or a steep swing — the leading edge digs

Grind

Grind is where material has been removed from the heel, toe or trailing edge of the sole. More relief lets you open the face without the leading edge lifting off the turf, which is what makes flop shots and short-sided lobs possible. Less relief gives you a wider, more supportive sole. If you’re not routinely opening the face, a full or mid sole will serve you better than a heavily ground one.

09 Set makeup and gapping the top of the bag

You’re allowed 14 clubs. The job is to cover every distance from your wedge floor to your driver with even gaps, and the place that always goes wrong is the top of the bag.

The honest test for any long iron: from a flat fairway lie, do you hit it well at least half the time? If not, that slot belongs to a hybrid or a fairway wood. A hybrid launches higher, holds greens better and is far easier out of rough. The only thing you give up is the ability to flight it down.

13 What this tool can’t do

This is a static fitting. It measures your body and asks about your tendencies. That’s genuinely how a professional fitting starts, and for length, lie, grip size and set makeup it gets most golfers to within one increment of the right answer.

What it can’t see:

  • Your posture and shaft droop at impact. Only a lie board and impact tape reveal these, and they are why a dynamic fit can land a full step away from the static one.
  • Your actual launch, spin and dispersion. Shaft profile — where a shaft is stiff along its length, and its torque — matters as much as the letter on it, and only a launch monitor can read it.
  • Whether your ball flight is a swing fault or an equipment fault. A slice caused by an open clubface won’t be fixed by an upright lie angle. Equipment can reduce a curve; it can’t remove the cause.
  • Anything about a swing that’s actively changing. If you’re mid-rebuild with a coach, fit the swing you’ll have, not the one you’ve this month.

Use this to walk into a fitting knowing what to ask for, and to sanity-check what you’re told.
